CONTINGENCY PROCEDURE

Primary Autopilot → Manual Hydraulic Steering → Rudder Failure → Emergency Signalling → Anchoring → Safe Sheltered Anchorage

Sextant Protocol Doctrine – Resilience

Purpose

This document describes a high-level maritime contingency and resilience progression for a vessel experiencing progressive loss of normal navigation and steering capability.

It is intended for:

- training and resilience analysis;
- failure-mode and V&V discussion;
- simulator scenario design;
- human-decision and emergency-response analysis.

It is not a substitute for the vessel's Safety Management System (SMS), emergency procedures, Master's standing orders, COLREGs, flag-state requirements, class requirements, or manufacturer instructions.

The Master/person in command remains the final operational authority.

---

1. NORMAL CONDITION — PRIMARY AUTOPILOT

Initial condition

The vessel is operating normally with:

- primary navigation available;
- autopilot available;
- steering system available;
- propulsion available;
- communications available;
- navigation lights and required equipment operational.

The first principle is:

«Do not wait for total failure before recognising degradation.»

Observe changes in:

- steering response;
- autopilot performance;
- heading stability;
- propulsion;
- hydraulic pressure;
- electrical power;
- navigation references;
- communications;
- weather and sea conditions.

---

2. PRIMARY AUTOPILOT FAILURE

If the primary autopilot becomes unreliable or fails:

Immediate resilience principle

Do not allow the failed automation to remain the assumed source of control.

The bridge team should:

1. Recognise and verify the failure.
2. Inform the Master/person in command as required.
3. Establish manual steering or the vessel's approved alternate steering mode.
4. Maintain safe heading and situational awareness.
5. Assess traffic, weather, hazards and available sea room.
6. Notify relevant parties as required by the vessel's procedures.
7. Record the failure and actions taken.

Resilience transition

AUTOPILOT → HUMAN CONTROL

The objective is not to restore automation immediately.

The objective is:

«Maintain control of the vessel safely while the failure is assessed.»

---

3. MANUAL HYDRAULIC STEERING

Where the vessel's approved steering arrangement provides manual/hydraulic steering:

Transition

The bridge team establishes the approved alternate steering method in accordance with vessel procedures.

The Master/team verifies:

- steering response;
- rudder indication;
- hydraulic availability;
- communications between bridge and steering-control location;
- propulsion status;
- surrounding traffic;
- navigational position.

The vessel is now operating in a degraded-control condition.

Principle

«Manual control is a resilience layer, not an assumption that the failure has been solved.»

Continue monitoring for secondary failures.

---

4. RUDDER / STEERING FAILURE

If effective rudder control is subsequently lost:

Situation changes

The vessel may no longer be able to maintain the intended heading.

The response therefore changes from:

STEERING RECOVERY

to:

VESSEL CONTROL + COLLISION AVOIDANCE + SAFE-STATE MANAGEMENT

The Master/team should:

1. Verify the steering failure.
2. Determine the vessel's actual movement and heading.
3. Assess traffic and immediate collision risk.
4. Use all available approved means to warn other vessels.
5. Establish communications as appropriate.
6. Assess propulsion and remaining manoeuvring capability.
7. Determine whether the vessel can reach a safer area.
8. Prepare anchoring and other emergency measures where appropriate.

---

5. EMERGENCY VISUAL / LIGHT SIGNALS

Where normal electrical/navigation-light capability is impaired, the vessel should use the approved emergency signalling arrangements carried by the vessel, consistent with applicable regulations and the Master's/SMS procedures.

Historically, vessels have used manually operated lamps or oil lanterns as emergency means of maintaining visibility/signalling when normal systems were unavailable.

The principle is:

«Loss of automation or electrical capability must not become loss of situational visibility.»

Any emergency lighting or signalling must be used in accordance with applicable maritime requirements and vessel procedures.

---

6. ANCHOR AS A SAFE-STATE MEASURE

If steering or propulsion capability becomes insufficient to maintain a safe situation, anchoring may become an important contingency option where depth, seabed, traffic, weather, local restrictions and vessel characteristics make it safe and practicable.

The decision belongs to the Master/person in command.

Before anchoring, assess:

- available depth;
- seabed suitability;
- weather and sea state;
- wind/current;
- nearby traffic;
- underwater and surface hazards;
- proximity to shore;
- swinging room;
- charted restrictions;
- anchor and cable condition;
- remaining propulsion/steering capability.

Objective

«Move from uncontrolled drift toward a controlled safe state where practicable.»

---

7. PAYING OUT ADDITIONAL ANCHOR CABLE / CHAIN

If anchoring is selected, the amount of cable/chain paid out must be determined by the Master and crew using:

- vessel-specific procedures;
- depth;
- anchor type;
- seabed;
- weather;
- wind/current;
- expected holding requirements;
- available scope;
- local conditions.

Do not use a universal fixed length from this document.

The crew should monitor:

- anchor holding;
- vessel position;
- heading;
- cable direction/tension;
- surrounding traffic;
- changes in wind/current;
- dragging or loss of holding.

Critical distinction

Dropping the anchor is not the same as establishing a safe anchorage.

The vessel must be monitored to verify that the anchor is holding and that the resulting position remains safe.

---

8. DRAGGING / LOSS OF HOLDING

If the vessel is dragging anchor or the anchor is not providing the required holding:

Reassess immediately.

The Master/team should consider the vessel's approved contingency options, which may include:

- additional appropriate anchoring measures;
- controlled use of remaining propulsion;
- tug assistance where available;
- emergency communications;
- movement toward a safer location where practicable.

The response must be based on the actual vessel condition and local circumstances.

---

9. TRANSITION TOWARD SAFE SHELTERED ANCHORAGE

If sufficient control remains to move the vessel toward a safer sheltered anchorage, the objective is:

HAZARDOUS CONDITION → CONTROLLED MOVEMENT → SAFE SHELTERED AREA → STABLE ANCHORED CONDITION

The Master should continuously reassess:

- weather;
- traffic;
- depth;
- position;
- anchor holding;
- steering;
- propulsion;
- communications;
- crew readiness;
- proximity to hazards.

The destination is not simply a geographic location.

The destination is a stable and controllable vessel state.

---

10. RESILIENCE PROGRESSION

The complete failure progression can therefore be represented as:

NORMAL OPERATION
       ↓
PRIMARY AUTOPILOT FAILURE
       ↓
VERIFY FAILURE
       ↓
MANUAL / HYDRAULIC STEERING
       ↓
STEERING / RUDDER FAILURE
       ↓
DEGRADED MANOEUVRABILITY
       ↓
EMERGENCY COMMUNICATION / SIGNALLING
       ↓
ASSESS ANCHORING OPTION
       ↓
ANCHOR DEPLOYMENT
       ↓
VERIFY ANCHOR HOLDING
       ↓
PAY OUT ADDITIONAL CABLE/CHAIN AS REQUIRED
       ↓
MONITOR FOR DRAGGING
       ↓
CONTROLLED MOVEMENT WHERE PRACTICABLE
       ↓
SAFE SHELTERED ANCHORAGE
       ↓
STABLE / DEGRADED SAFE STATE

---

11. SEXTANT RESILIENCE INTERPRETATION

This scenario demonstrates the Sextant principle:

Failure is not an isolated event.

A primary failure can create dependencies and expose additional vulnerabilities.

Therefore:

ENVIRONMENT
    ↓
FAILURE
    ↓
DEPENDENCY
    ↓
CASCADE
    ↓
REDUNDANCY
    ↓
HUMAN INTERVENTION
    ↓
DEGRADED STATE
    ↓
SAFE STATE

The system should continuously distinguish between:

- an environmental condition;
- a confirmed technical failure;
- a dependency;
- a cascade;
- an available redundancy;
- a human intervention requirement;
- a degraded state;
- a safe or unsafe final condition.

---

12. HUMAN AUTHORITY

This contingency model does not authorise autonomous execution.

The system may:

- observe;
- verify;
- assess;
- identify dependencies;
- identify possible progression;
- present options;
- record the resulting state.

The Master/person in command decides and acts according to the vessel's approved procedures.

Golden Rule

OBSERVE → VERIFY → ASSESS → DECIDE → ACT → UPDATE

Human authority remains final.

---

13. SAFETY BOUNDARY

This README is a research, training and V&V representation of contingency progression.

It does not:

- issue commands to a vessel;
- control an autopilot;
- control steering;
- operate propulsion;
- deploy an anchor;
- replace bridge procedures;
- replace the Master;
- replace the vessel's SMS;
- provide navigation instructions for a particular voyage.

Any real-world implementation must be reviewed against the applicable vessel, equipment, regulations, operating area and approved emergency procedures.

---

Core Resilience Principle

«When automation fails, resilience is the ability to recognise the failure, preserve human control, transition through available layers of redundancy, prevent escalation where possible, and move the vessel toward the safest achievable state.»
